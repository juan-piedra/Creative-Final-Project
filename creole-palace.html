Creole Palace

The Creole Palace was the nightclub of the Douglas Hotel in downtown San Diego. It opened with the hotel in 1924, and over the next few decades it became a regular stop for some of the biggest names in Black music. People called it the “Cotton Club of the West.”

More than a jazz club

BlackPast describes the Creole Palace as “a popular, high energy cabaret that catered primarily to the African American population of San Diego.” Its owners “didn’t intend for it to be simply a jazz club,” and it wasn’t one. Historian Leland Saito says the club was well known for “jazz/blues, boogie-woogie, Charleston music and dancing,” and BlackPast calls it “a cabaret with a light flavoring of burlesque.” By the 1930s, BlackPast says, it was “the most famous western cabaret outside of Los Angeles.”

The San Diego Reader reports two shows a night, at 11:00 p.m. and 1:00 a.m., each with different acts, and a 25-cent admission. Patrons danced the Lindy Hop, the Black Bottom, the Shimmy and the Susie Q. The Reader says the owners patterned the club after Harlem’s Cotton Club, which had a whites-only policy. The Douglas did not. BlackPast adds that while national burlesque was “strictly segregated,” the Creole Palace “managed to offer much of the same entertainment to its customers of all races.”

Who played there

The course reading on San Diego’s Black history shows the hotel and club in a photo captioned “the stop for Bessie Smith, Billie Holiday, the Mills Brothers, Duke Ellington.” The same reading also names Louis Armstrong, and BlackPast adds Count Basie. The Reader says that during the 1930s and ’40s the “Chittlin Circuit,” the string of venues where Black performers could play during segregation, always stopped in San Diego. BlackPast also names Sonny Criss, Hampton Hawes and Erroll Garner among the jazz artists who “put in time” at the Creole.

The working musicians

BlackPast says the club “provided a home and launching pad for their slightly less famous but no less important colleagues.” One of them was Froebel “Fro” Brigham, “a gifted musician” who worked at the Creole “off and on for over 20 years.” Saito says Brigham managed the club in the 1940s and 1950s, and he quotes Brigham remembering, “People came from L.A. just to visit the Creole Palace.”

BlackPast singles out Harold Land, one of the most compelling artists to come from the West Coast, who “began working at the Creole Palace just after he left high school.” He was part of the band Brigham organized.

The course reading puts the club at the center of a larger community: “If the Douglas Hotel and Creole Palace were the cultural heart of Black San Diego, Logan Heights was its home.”

Music, memory and joy

The Music as Resistance reading says that Black music “has made room for resistance and joy at the same time.” It also explains why a place like this matters to history: “A government document might tell us what happened. A song can tell us how people experienced it: what they feared, what they hoped for, what they loved, and what they refused to accept.” Jazz, the reading adds, carried “the freedom to improvise, experiment, and create.”

The Creole Palace shows what a night out meant to people living under segregation. The Reader describes Thursday as “Kitchen Mechanics’ Night,” when Black women who worked as maids at the Douglas got the night off and could get in for free.

A complicated place

Not everyone approved of the club. The Reader says many in the Black community saw the Douglas as a “place of sin.” The sources also disagree about who filled the room. BlackPast says the club served mainly Black San Diegans, while the Reader says weekend prices created a mostly white audience for the floor shows. The sources don’t settle this, so this page doesn’t either. It fits the Black Joy reading’s warning not to confuse Black joy with forced positivity: the joy here was real, and it was not tidy.

How long the music lasted

BlackPast says the Creole Palace “remained the center of African American entertainment in San Diego from 1924 until the post-World War II era,” and its page title gives the years 1924 to 1946. Other sources run later. Saito says Brigham managed the club in the 1940s and 1950s, and the Reader says “By the late 1950s, the glory days were gone.” The hotel was torn down in 1985.
