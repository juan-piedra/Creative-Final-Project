Black Arts & Culture District

The San Diego Black Arts & Culture District runs along Imperial Avenue between 61st and 69th Streets in Encanto, in Southeast San Diego. The course reading calls it “a nine-block corridor of Black-owned businesses, art spaces, and community gathering places, sometimes described historically as a ‘mini Black Wall Street.’” Its story picks up where the Douglas Hotel and Creole Palace pages leave off.

Where the community rebuilt

The course reading says the 1985 demolition of the Douglas Hotel came from the same redevelopment pressure that “helped push San Diego’s Black community into Southeast San Diego.” In its reading on the district, it says that after redlining and later highway construction and redevelopment displaced San Diego’s original downtown Black cultural district, “the community rebuilt in Southeast San Diego, particularly Logan Heights, Encanto, and Valencia Park, the very neighborhoods marked ‘hazardous’ on the 1936 HOLC map.”

The Redlining reading quotes what the 1936 appraisers wrote about Logan Heights: “Homes show only slight degree of pride of ownership and are on the average negligently maintained.” Today the district stands in the same part of the city those appraisers marked down.

Imperial Avenue

The district’s website says Imperial Avenue, originally U.S. Highway 80, “was the commercial corridor for Encanto.” Because of restrictive covenants, it says, “Black residents and businesses were limited to neighborhoods south of Market Street,” like Logan Heights and Grant Hills. Over time, Black-owned businesses moved east along Imperial. The site names Anderson Ragsdale Mortuary, the Lighthouse Newspaper, Gilliam Fish Market, Sister Pee Wee’s Soul Food, Sidney Cooper’s Twilight Barber Shop, and Cooper and Son’s Fresh Fruits & Vegetables.

Making it official

The district was a long time coming. The site says it builds on an earlier plan started by the late Council members Charles Lewis and Rev. George Stevens. NBC 7 reported in April 2022 that Dajahn Blevins, who heads the arts program San Diego Urban Warriors, had been pushing for an arts and culture district for 30 years.

On June 14, 2022, the district’s site says, Council President Pro Tempore Monica Montgomery Steppe secured the City Council’s unanimous approval of Resolution R-2022-508, which established the district. The site describes her vision as “a central location where all can celebrate the history, arts, and culture of Black people.” It also names the San Diego African American Museum of Fine Art (SDAAMFA) as a partner working with the Council District 4 office. The course reading describes SDAAMFA as founded in 1973 by Dr. Shirley Day Williams and operating as a “museum without walls.”

What the district is for

The district’s stated mission is “To establish a Black arts and culture renaissance that revitalizes Black hospitality, economic empowerment, and commerce.” The word hospitality points back to the Douglas Hotel, which the course reading calls “the only downtown San Diego hotel offering quality lodging to Black travelers during segregation.”

Artist Maxx Moses told NBC 7 what kind of street he pictures: “New York has Harlem’s 125th Street. You can walk up and down and see vendors, stores, blah, blah, blah.” Harlem comes up again here. It is also the nickname the course reading gives the Douglas Hotel and Creole Palace: “Harlem of the West.”

Joy on Imperial Avenue

The course reading names three spaces in the district: The Block Club, Marie Widman Park, “named for a longtime local NAACP leader and city planning advocate,” and Graffiti Gardens. The district’s 2026 calendar shows what goes on around them. It lists the Encanto Block Party Music Festival at Marie Widman Park on Sunday, July 12, 2026, as “A family friendly FREE event” with food, vendors and activities. It lists a book club, a wine and honey tasting and a yarn art night at The Block Club. It also lists a Harlem of the West Jazz Night on April 18, 2026, at the San Diego History Center.

This is what the Black Joy reading means when it says “Black people have always created spaces, relationships, music, art, humor, beauty, and celebration—even under conditions designed to deny them freedom and dignity.” The institutions reading puts it this way: “Communities actively keep culture alive by creating spaces to gather, tell stories, make art, educate others, and celebrate their experiences.”

Rebuilding is not finished

The course reading is direct about today’s challenges: “Black San Diego’s population and visible cultural footprint have shrunk in recent decades.” 10News reported in August 2025 that there used to be “more than 200 Black-owned businesses” and that today “a local nonprofit has counted just over 30 in the area.” It also described a 1960s business directory showing “multiple businesses on each page, ranging from grocery stores to dentists and doctors.” In its Emerald Hills case study, the course reading says of that neighborhood’s housing history, “That history is not settled.”

Still, people are staying. Daneyel Walker, who owns The Mental Bar in the district, told 10News, “we’ve been here three years and we’re not going anywhere.” The Black Joy reading says Black joy can mean “knowing exactly what is wrong and choosing to create, celebrate, love, laugh, dance, or dream anyway.”
