Douglas Hotel

A place to stay when most doors were closed

In 1852, a San Diego grand jury demanded that black men be “compelled to leave” the city. Subsequent “restrictive covenants” (limitations placed in contracts) effectively forbade black families from living in most of the city. The Douglas was an exception. Sociologist Leland Saito notes that it was known as “a welcoming safe haven and an entertainment mecca,” and that it rented rooms to both black and white guests.

The Douglas had a restaurant, a card room, a barbershop, billiard rooms and a dry cleaner, in addition to its own nightclub, the Creole Palace. It was also a major employer; as one source quoted in the article noted, the jobs were well paid and secure.

Who built it

The hotel was built in 1924 by George Ramsey and Robert Rowe. It seems that the latter died shortly after the opening, and Mabel Rowe took over her husband’s work. According to the University of San Diego student researchers, the hotel was named after Frederick Douglass, the ex-slave and human rights activist.

Joy on their own terms

The Douglas’ nightclub, the Creole Palace, was where the music happened. The course reading on the history of San Diego’s black population refers to the Douglas as “proof that even under segregation, Black San Diegans built spaces of joy and self-determination on their own terms.”

The Black Joy reading includes a passage that puts this in perspective: “Black people have always created spaces, relationships, music, art, humor, beauty, and celebration—even under conditions designed to deny them freedom and dignity.” Black joy as resistance, it says, “can mean knowing exactly what is wrong and choosing to create, celebrate, love, laugh, dance, or dream anyway.” The Douglas was a business, but it served as a sanctuary where black residents could unwind and enjoy music in an era when they were otherwise not permitted to be in downtown San Diego.

What happened to it

Saito explains that post-WWII saw a growing economy, and patterns of segregation and suburbanization changed where black San Diegans lived and worked. As one source notes, “By the late 1950s the glory days were gone.” The hotel was sold in 1956.

It became a casualty of the downtown redevelopment in the 1980s. In 1984, the Centre City Development Corporation described the block as “extremely blighted” and proposed a photo exhibit to commemorate the early history of the Douglas. Saito observes that while many residents were interested in preserving it, “no major lobbying effort occurred.” Larry Malone of the San Diego Historical Society said community leaders were occupied with issues in southeast San Diego and not focused on historic preservation downtown. The Douglas was demolished in 1985 to make way for housing and commercial developments. Malone summed up the situation succinctly: “We lost a jewel.”

The course readings put the loss of the Douglas in context. One noted that it was demolished “to make way for downtown redevelopment, the same redevelopment pressure that, as covered on the Redlining page, helped push San Diego’s Black community into Southeast San Diego.” Another discusses how, after “redlining and later highway construction and redevelopment displaced San Diego’s original downtown Black cultural district, the community rebuilt in Southeast San Diego,” in neighborhoods marked “hazardous” on the 1936 HOLC map. This relates to the Black Arts & Culture District project because it is a continuation of that legacy.
