Douglas Hotel

The Douglas Hotel opened in 1924 at Second Avenue and Market Street in downtown San Diego, in what is now the Gaslamp Quarter. It was Black-owned, and for decades it was the only downtown hotel offering quality lodging to Black travelers during segregation. People called it the “Harlem of the West.” It was torn down in 1985.

A place to stay when most doors were closed

In 1852, a San Diego grand jury ruled that black men should be “compelled to leave” the city. Subsequent “restrictive covenants” (limitations placed in contracts) effectively forbade black families from living in most of the city. The Douglas was an exception. Historian Leland Saito notes that it was known as “a welcoming safe haven and an entertainment mecca,” and that it rented rooms to both black and white guests.

The Douglas had a restaurant, a card room, a barbershop, billiard rooms and a dry cleaner, in addition to its own nightclub, the Creole Palace. It was also a major employer, as one source quoted in the article noted, the jobs being “good paying and secure.”

Who built it

The hotel was built in 1924 by George Ramsey and Robert Rowe. It seems that the latter died shortly after the opening, and Mabel Rowe took over her husband’s share of the business from her husband’s estate. According to the University of San Diego student researchers, the hotel was named after Frederick Douglass, the ex-slave and human rights activist.

Joy on their own terms

The Douglas’ nightclub, the Creole Palace, is where the music happened. The course reading on the history of San Diego’s black population refers to the Douglas as “proof that despite segregation, black San Diegans carved out places and spaces in which to hold serve their own communities, gather in celebration and in joy on their own terms.”

The Black Joy reading includes a passage that puts this in perspective: “As a result, they have created in spite of the oppression, their own spaces, relationships, music, art, humor, beauty, and celebration. When Black joy is expressed as a resistance, it often serves as a reminder that despite knowing exactly what is wrong, we continue to create, to celebrate, to love, to laugh, to dance, and to dream.” The Douglas was a business, but it served as a sanctuary where black residents could unwind and enjoy music in an era when they were otherwise not permitted to be in downtown San Diego.

What happened to it

Saito explains that post-WWII saw the growth of a middle class, and patterns of segregation and suburbanization changed where black San Diegans lived and worked. “Prior to WWII, many black San Diegans were employed in defense industries and found few restrictions in the communities in which they lived,” he writes. “However, the shifting patterns of segregation as well as the rise of new suburbs have resulted in a different demographic makeup of the region today. As one source notes, ‘By the mid-1950s the glory days [of the Douglas] were over.’ The hotel was sold in 1956.

It became a casualty of the downtown redevelopment in the 1980s. The 1984 redevelopment commission’s “plan for the block noted that it was extremely blighted and proposed a photo exhibit to commemorate the early history of the Douglas.” Saito observes that while many residents were interested in its legacy as a cultural landmark, “no organized lobbying efforts occurred to save the building. When Larry Malone of the San Diego Historical Society was asked about this he stated that it may have had much to do with the fact that ‘the people who could have done something about it were busy with issues in the southeast and not interested in historic preservation downtown.’” The Douglas was demolished in 1985 to make way for housing and commercial developments. Malone summed up the situation succinctly: “We lost a jewel.”

The course readings put the theft of the Douglas in context. One noted that it was demolished “as part of the downtown redevelopment, the same process that, as discussed on the Redlining page, pushed San Diego’s black community to the Southeast.” Another discusses how despite “the effects of redlining and later highway construction and redevelopment,” San Diegans “recreated a black cultural district in the Southeast” because of the “hazardous” designation by the FHA in the 1936. This relates to the Black Arts & Culture District project because it is a continuation of that legacy.
