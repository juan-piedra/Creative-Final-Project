Douglas Hotel

A place to stay when most doors were closed

In 1852, a San Diego grand jury demanded that Black men be “compelled to leave” the town. Later, restrictive covenants, clauses written into property deeds, kept Black families out of most of the city’s neighborhoods. The Douglas was different. Historian Leland Saito writes that it was described as “a welcoming safe haven and an entertainment mecca,” and that it rented rooms to both Black and white guests.

The hotel had a restaurant, a card room, a barbershop, billiard rooms and a dry cleaner, plus its own nightclub, the Creole Palace. It was also a major employer. One account Saito cites describes the jobs as well paid and secure.

Who built it

George Ramsey and Robert Rowe built the hotel in 1924. Rowe died several months after it opened, and his widow, Mabel Rowe, took over his work. A University of San Diego student research project says the owners named the hotel after Frederick Douglass, the formerly enslaved activist.

Joy on their own terms

The hotel’s nightclub, the Creole Palace, is where the music happened. The course reading on San Diego’s Black history calls the Douglas “proof that even under segregation, Black San Diegans built spaces of joy and self-determination on their own terms.”

The Black Joy reading explains why that matters: “Black people have always created spaces, relationships, music, art, humor, beauty, and celebration—even under conditions designed to deny them freedom and dignity.” It adds that Black joy as resistance “can mean knowing exactly what is wrong and choosing to create, celebrate, love, laugh, dance, or dream anyway.” The Douglas was a business, but it was also a place where Black travelers and locals could rest, eat and enjoy live music at a time when much of the city was closed to them.

What happened to it

Saito explains that after World War II, a growing economy, changing patterns of segregation and suburbanization drew Black San Diegans toward new neighborhoods and businesses. He quotes one source who says that by the late 1950s “the glory days were gone.” The hotel was sold in 1956.

By the 1980s the building was in the way of downtown redevelopment. In 1984, city redevelopment documents called the block “extremely blighted” and proposed a photo display about the hotel’s early days. Saito reports that although city residents showed interest in saving the building, no major lobbying effort took place. Larry Malone of the San Diego Historical Society said community leaders were “too busy with issues in southeast San Diego to focus on historic preservation downtown.” The Douglas was torn down in 1985 to make way for housing and commercial space. Malone put it simply: “We lost a jewel.”

The course readings place this loss in a larger story. One says the building was demolished “to make way for downtown redevelopment, the same redevelopment pressure that, as covered on the Redlining page, helped push San Diego’s Black community into Southeast San Diego.” Another says that after “redlining and later highway construction and redevelopment displaced San Diego’s original downtown Black cultural district, the community rebuilt in Southeast San Diego,” in neighborhoods marked “hazardous” on a 1936 federal housing map. That rebuilding is where the Black Arts & Culture District comes in.
